## How he writes to Claude

This is him with no audience and no editing. It is the best evidence of his natural compression.

Read it for compression and impatience only. These are instructions to a tool, not prose - maximally elliptical, articles dropped, no structure. Calibrating published writing against this section is how you end up producing clipped fragments that read as curt rather than direct. The LinkedIn and strategy samples below are the register targets; this section tells you how much padding he will tolerate, which is none.

> Less fluffy.. see Apify style guide. Get straight to the point.

> Hmm.. that misses quite a lot from my past and background. Do a little bit of a research on me first.

> Here is my draft. Apply Apify Style Guide (from Notion), improve the writing while keeping my tone of voice.

> How does it read as something I'd wrote considering our past chats and history?

> Use Apify.

> Do we have somewhere stored my personal writing style?

Notes: sentences average under ten words. The ".." pause appears constantly. "Hmm.." as a soft disagreement opener. Occasional dropped article ("a little bit of a research") and slipped tense ("something I'd wrote") - these are speech patterns, not things to imitate in published prose, but they tell you how fast he is moving.

**ELC mentoring, his draft:**

> Six mentoring sessions usually take me longer.. last week, it all happened in two hours. "What? 20 minutes each?!" you might ask. From both sides, it is challenging whether you are a mentee or a mentor in my case.
>
> Attendees of Engineering Leadership Conference '26 had a chance to book a timeslot and open up about their challenges. I had yet again realised how broad the area of DATA can be: from managing data science / ML teams, through building data products in era of AI, all the way to limits of scaling data architecture in Databricks.
>

Structure worth reusing: concrete opener with a number, reader-directed aside in quotes, a scope-showing ladder (from X, through Y, all the way to Z), named credit to organizers, personal gratitude close. Three paragraphs, no CTA.

## Email to a candidate

His own writing, more formal register. Context: a candidate withdrew from a hiring process with critical feedback about the take-home assignment.

> You raise fair points. An "optional" task in an interview context doesn't truly feel optional, and that's a gap in how we framed it, not in how you read it.
>
> "ML models always give you an answer, the hard part is evaluating whether that answer is useful." Well said. That's a mindset I value.

What works and is characteristic: accepts the criticism in three words and moves on. Puts the blame in the right place with an explicit correction ("not in how you read it"). Quotes the other person's own line back and endorses it in four words.

What did not work in the same email and should be cut in future drafts: "I genuinely appreciate you taking the time to share it rather than simply stepping away", "Your feedback will shape how we present this assignment going forward", "Should your circumstances or interests change down the road, don't hesitate to reach out."

## Strategy and internal docs

His tightest register. Claim, scope, outcome. No warm-up.

Strategy challenge statement he wrote for the data team:

> Apify will be the company where every team ships faster because data is instant, trusted, and self-service - giving us a decision-speed advantage no competitor in web data can match.

Session abstract, after editing:

> We're going all in on AI agents and MCP, but agents are only as good as the data they can access. If metrics are ambiguous, pipelines are fragile, or data isn't discoverable, agents will inherit all of that at scale.

Note the conditional-then-consequence move in the second one, and that both are single sentences doing a lot of work. He also writes explicit "not doing" lists in strategy docs - naming what is out of scope is part of how he communicates, not an afterthought.

## Recurring tics

Preserve these:

- `..` as a mid-sentence breath (render as `...` when published)
- `Hmm..` to open a disagreement
- Rhetorical question in quotes, aimed at the reader
- ALL CAPS on one word for punch
- "yet again" when noting a repeated realization
- Ladder constructions: from X, through Y, all the way to Z
- Naming people in full, with their role or what they built
- Parenthetical self-corrections mid-sentence

Fix these:

- British spellings
- Dropped or wrong articles
- Present perfect where simple past belongs
- Slashes standing in for "and"
- Czech word order in longer English sentences
